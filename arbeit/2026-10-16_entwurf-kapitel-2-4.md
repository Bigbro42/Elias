## 2 Theoretical Background

### 2.1 Artificial Intelligence in Organizational Work

The OECD defines an AI system as \"a machine-based system that is
capable of influencing the environment by producing an output
(predictions, recommendations or decisions) for a given set of
objectives\" and adds that such systems \"are designed to operate with
varying levels of autonomy\" (OECD, 2023, p. 94). AI itself is not new,
but earlier forms were mostly rule-based, relied on explicitly
programmed logical rules and were aimed primarily at expert users
(Oksanen & Savolainen, 2026, p. 4). Current AI differs from earlier
digital technologies in three respects: it extends the range of tasks
that can be automated beyond routine, non-cognitive tasks, it is a
general-purpose technology that affects nearly every sector and
occupation, and it develops at unprecedented speed (OECD, 2023, p. 14).
Generative AI has intensified this development. Dell\'Acqua et al.
(2023, p. 1) describe it as an entirely new category of automation,
because its abilities overlap with those of the most creative, most
educated and most highly paid workers.

Whether AI replaces or supports human work is, however, not determined
by the technology alone. Raisch and Krakowski (2021, p. 192) distinguish
automation, in which machines take over a human task, from augmentation,
in which humans collaborate closely with machines to perform it. A
pre-registered field experiment with 758 consultants of Boston
Consulting Group illustrates what this means in practice (Dell\'Acqua et
al., 2023, pp. 5--6). For tasks within the capabilities of GPT-4,
consultants with AI access completed on average 12.5% more subtasks and
produced responses of more than 40% higher quality (Dell\'Acqua et al.,
2023, p. 12). For a task outside this \"jagged technological frontier\"
(Dell\'Acqua et al., 2023, p. 1), however, consultants using AI produced
a correct solution on average 19 percentage points less often than those
without AI (Dell\'Acqua et al., 2023, p. 14). The value of AI therefore
depends not only on the tool but also on the employee who decides
whether, when and how far to rely on it.

For organizations, this means that introducing AI does not guarantee its
use. A multilevel review describes the debate about AI at work as highly
polarized between expectations of significant job losses and of higher
productivity and better job quality (Bankins, Ocampo, Marrone, Restubog
& Woo, 2024, p. 160). Employees form their own view of the technology,
and the review notes that more positive attitudes are likely to lead to
efforts to upskill and to use AI, whereas more negative attitudes are
likely to lead to resistance and poor uptake (Bankins et al., 2024, p.
160). The next section therefore turns to research on technology
acceptance and to behavioral intention as the outcome variable of this
thesis.

### 2.2 Technology Acceptance and Behavioral Intention

Research on technology acceptance asks why individuals adopt or reject a
new technology. Its most influential framework, the technology
acceptance model (TAM), explains adoption through two beliefs (Oksanen &
Savolainen, 2026, p. 36). Perceived usefulness is \"the degree to which
a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or
her job performance\", and perceived ease of use is \"the degree to
which a person believes that using a particular system would be free of
effort\" (Davis, 1989, p. 320). The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use
of Technology (UTAUT) later combined eight earlier models; its central
predictor, performance expectancy, integrates constructs such as
perceived usefulness and the outcome expectations of social cognitive
theory (Venkatesh, Morris, Davis & Davis, 2003, p. 447).

In both models, behavioral intention is the direct antecedent of use.
Venkatesh et al. (2003, p. 427) describe the role of intention as a
predictor of usage as \"critical\" and \"well-established\", and user
acceptance has accordingly been defined as \"the behavioural intention
or willingness to use, buy, or try a good or service\" (Kelly, Kaye &
Oviedo-Trespalacios, 2023, p. 2). This thesis uses behavioral intention
to use AI as its outcome variable for two reasons. First, AI use is
still developing: only a minority of the Austrian population has used
generative AI (see Section 2.7), and even for employees who have tried
AI, whether it becomes a regular part of their work is still open.
Intention captures this decision while it is being made. Second, a
cross-sectional survey can measure intention, but it cannot observe
later use. This choice has a cost: views formed before use should not be
confused with actual use (Kelly et al., 2023, p. 29), a limitation that
Chapter 4 takes up again. Because all respondents have used AI for their
work at least once (Section 4.3), behavioral intention refers here to
future use of AI at work, which may mean continued, renewed or expanded
use rather than first-time adoption. The term adoption intention in the
title of this thesis is used in this broader sense.

Applied to AI, the classic models remain useful but incomplete. A
systematic review of AI acceptance found that TAM was the most
frequently used theory, but that most authors extended it with further
variables, most often attitudes and trust (Kelly et al., 2023, pp.
8--9). The authors conclude that \"traditional base theories matter, but
idiosyncrasies, such as trust and attitudes, must be captured to
understand user acceptance completely\" (Kelly et al., 2023, p. 29). The
same review reports a survey of public attitudes in 28 European
countries in which the perceived threat of job loss due to AI negatively
predicted acceptance of AI (Vu & Lim, 2021, as cited in Kelly et al.,
2023, p. 10), and a review of AI adoption in organizations lists fear of
job loss, anxiety, perceived risk and trust among the factors that shape
employees\' adoption (Khanfar, Kiani Mavi, Iranmanesh & Gengatharen,
2025, p. 3735). Self-efficacy also has a place in this tradition,
although a contested one. UTAUT modeled it only as an indirect
determinant of intention, and its direct effect disappeared as users
gained experience with a system (Venkatesh et al., 2003, pp. 446, 455).

This thesis therefore does not test TAM or UTAUT as a whole. It takes
behavioral intention from this tradition and explains it through two
AI-specific evaluations, trust in AI and AI-induced job insecurity, with
AI self-efficacy as their common antecedent. Why a belief about one\'s
own capability should influence both evaluations is explained by social
cognitive theory, which the next section introduces.

### 2.3 Social Cognitive Theory and Self-Efficacy

Social cognitive theory provides the theoretical foundation of this
thesis. It explains behavior through triadic reciprocal causation, in
which personal factors, behavior and the environment influence one
another (Bandura, 1997, pp. 5--6). People are therefore shaped by their
circumstances, but they also exercise some control over them (Bandura,
1997, p. 8). This view fits the study of workplace AI, because employees
who work with the same technology may still interpret it and act on it
in different ways. Social cognitive theory is accordingly among the
frameworks used to explain workers\' attitudes toward AI (Bankins et
al., 2024, p. 162).

A central construct of social cognitive theory is self-efficacy, defined
as \"beliefs in one\'s capabilities to organize and execute the courses
of action required to produce given attainments\" (Bandura, 1997, p. 3).
Self-efficacy is thus a judgment of capability, not a forecast of
results. Bandura (1977, p. 193) separates efficacy expectations, the
conviction that one can carry out the required behavior, from outcome
expectations, the estimate that a behavior will lead to certain
outcomes. The two are not independent: where performance determines the
outcome, efficacy beliefs account for most of the variance in expected
outcomes (Bandura, 1997, p. 24). An employee who doubts their ability to
use AI well is therefore also likely to expect less from it.

Early technology acceptance research drew on this distinction. Davis
(1989, p. 321) grounded perceived ease of use partly in Bandura\'s work
on self-efficacy and described outcome judgments as similar to perceived
usefulness. Acceptance models, however, represented the efficacy
component mainly through evaluations of the system, whereas social
cognitive theory locates it in the person. The present study therefore
treats self-efficacy as a belief about oneself that is distinct from the
evaluation of the technology and models it as an antecedent of that
evaluation.

Self-efficacy has two functions that are central to the research model.
First, it guides behavior. People avoid situations they believe exceed
their coping skills and act with assurance in situations they judge
themselves capable of handling; efficacy expectations also determine how
much effort people invest and how long they persist when facing
obstacles (Bandura, 1977, p. 194). Second, it shapes how a situation is
appraised. Threat is not a fixed property of a situation but depends on
the match between perceived coping capabilities and potentially harmful
aspects of the environment (Bandura, 1997, p. 140). People with strong
efficacy beliefs therefore approach difficult tasks \"as challenges to
be mastered rather than as threats to be avoided\" (Bandura, 1997, p.
39). The same change can thus be experienced as an opportunity or as a
danger, depending on how capable a person feels of dealing with it.

Self-efficacy must not be equated with skills, because it concerns what
people believe they can do with their skills under varying circumstances
(Bandura, 1997, p. 37). Nor is it a fixed trait. Of its four sources
(Bandura, 1977, p. 195), mastery experiences are the most influential,
because they provide the most authentic evidence of whether one can
succeed (Bandura, 1997, p. 80). Compeau and Higgins (1995, p. 206) draw
the same conclusion for technology: the more successful interactions
individuals have with computers, the more likely they are to develop
high self-efficacy. Self-efficacy and technology use can therefore
reinforce each other.

Finally, self-efficacy is domain-specific. Efficacy beliefs form a
differentiated set of self-beliefs linked to distinct realms of
functioning (Bandura, 1997, p. 36), and measures must be tailored to the
domain of interest to have explanatory power (Bandura, 1997, pp. 40,
42). Information systems research followed this principle with computer
self-efficacy, a judgment of one\'s capability to use a computer
(Compeau & Higgins, 1995, p. 192). Marakas, Yi and Johnson (1998, pp.
128--129) further separated task-specific from general computer
self-efficacy and argued that the two cannot be used interchangeably.
The next section takes this logic one step further to AI self-efficacy.

### 2.4 AI Self-Efficacy

The domain specificity of self-efficacy raises the question of whether
measures of computer self-efficacy can simply be applied to AI. Several
arguments suggest that they cannot. Beyond the differences between AI
and earlier digital technologies described in Section 2.1, Wang and
Chuang (2024, p. 4787) point out that the capacity to make decisions
distinguishes AI from conventional computer programs and that people
with low AI self-efficacy may experience AI as more complicated and
stressful. Generative AI in particular is probabilistic and interactive,
which calls for a more nuanced conceptualization of efficacy than
traditional technology self-efficacy scales provide (Akben & Dong, 2025,
p. 5). Existing technology-related measures can therefore be applied to
AI only to a limited extent (Oksanen & Savolainen, 2026, p. 82), and an
AI-specific construct is required.

The literature conceptualizes AI self-efficacy in two ways. The first
focuses on using AI. Wang and Chuang (2024) define AI self-efficacy as
\"individuals\' general belief in their ability to use and interact with
AI\" (p. 4789). The second focuses on learning AI. Kim and Kim (2024, p.
8) measure employees\' confidence in their ability to learn AI
technology for their work, and Oksanen et al. (2026) define AI learning
readiness self-efficacy as \"an individual\'s belief in their ability to
learn and effectively utilize new AI technologies in daily life\" (p.
2).

This thesis focuses on the learning-oriented facet. AI self-efficacy is
understood as an employee\'s belief in their capability to learn new AI
technologies and use them effectively in their work. The definition
follows Oksanen et al. (2026, p. 2) and transfers it from daily life to
the work context. The learning orientation reflects the character of
current workplace AI: workers have to adapt to new tools promptly and
under uncertainty (Oksanen & Savolainen, 2026, p. 74), and Bandura
(1997, p. 187) already observed that increasing automation places more
weight on versatile skills that can be applied flexibly to rapidly
changing occupational demands. The belief that one can keep learning and
applying new AI tools is therefore more relevant than confidence in
operating one particular tool. The label AI self-efficacy is retained
because it is established in the literature and is also used for
learning-oriented measures (Kim & Kim, 2024, p. 8). The findings of this
study, however, refer to this learning-oriented facet and not to every
form of AI-related efficacy.

AI self-efficacy must be distinguished from adjacent constructs. It is
not the same as AI skills, because self-efficacy concerns beliefs about
what one can do with one\'s skills (see Section 2.3). It also differs
from AI literacy: whereas AI literacy and competence scales target
knowledge about AI and skill-based competence, AI learning readiness
self-efficacy measures the belief in one\'s capability to learn to use
AI (Oksanen et al., 2026, p. 7). As argued in Section 2.3, it is a
belief about the person, whereas perceived ease of use is an evaluation
of the system. Finally, it must be separated from behavioral intention
and from readiness. Self-efficacy items ask what a person can do, not
what they will do; perceived self-efficacy is a major determinant of
intention, but the two constructs are conceptually and empirically
separable (Bandura, 1997, p. 43). Likewise, self-efficacy captures
capability beliefs, whereas readiness captures preparedness to act
(Akben & Dong, 2025, p. 8). This distinction matters here, because the
scale used in this study carries the word readiness in its name but
measures capability beliefs.

The role of self-efficacy in acceptance research has shifted over time.
UTAUT did not retain it as a direct determinant of intention (see
Section 2.2), but research on AI has brought the construct back in
different roles. As a predictor, self-efficacy emerged from longitudinal
survey data on workers as a more robust predictor of AI use than
attitudes toward AI (Oksanen & Savolainen, 2026, p. 46). As a moderator,
self-efficacy in AI learning has been hypothesized to weaken the
negative effect of AI-induced job insecurity on psychological safety
(Kim & Kim, 2024, p. 6). As an outcome, weekly use of text-based AI
tools increased AI learning readiness self-efficacy within persons over
time (Oksanen et al., 2026, p. 6). Consistent with the role of mastery
experiences (Section 2.3), self-efficacy and AI use thus appear to
reinforce each other. The literature agrees that AI self-efficacy
matters, but not on where it sits in the process. The present study
models it as an antecedent of the employee\'s evaluation of AI; Chapter
3 justifies this choice, and Section 4.5 discusses its limits in a
cross-sectional design.

Three relevant operationalizations were identified in the literature
reviewed for this thesis. The scale by Wang and Chuang (2024, p. 4785)
matches the use-oriented definition, but with 22 items it is long for a
survey that measures four constructs, and it was validated mostly with
students (Wang & Chuang, 2024, p. 4791). The four-item scale by Kim and
Kim (2024, p. 8) is learning-oriented, but one of its items presupposes
organizational AI training. The AI Learning Readiness Self-Efficacy
scale (AILRSE-5) by Oksanen et al. (2026) matches the learning-oriented
definition adopted here with only five items and is therefore used; its
validation and adaptation are described in Section 4.2. In line with
Marakas et al. (1998, p. 154), the items ask about perceived ability
only, without reference to outcome expectations, and are phrased in
terms of capability (\"I\'m confident in my ability to ...\") rather
than intention.

### 2.5 Trust in AI

Section 2.4 described AI self-efficacy as a belief that employees hold
about themselves. Whether they are willing to work with AI, however,
also depends on what they believe about the technology. Trust becomes
relevant here because AI systems are often not transparent: their
decisions can be difficult to predict, and the logic behind a given
output is often poorly understood (Glikson & Woolley, 2020, p. 628).
Generative AI adds the difficulty that outputs may vary between attempts
and that systems change over time, which makes it hard to judge when
trust is warranted (Oksanen & Savolainen, 2026, p. 41). Employees who
use AI therefore regularly act on outputs they cannot fully verify, and
this is the kind of situation in which trust matters.

Most research on trust in AI builds on the definition by Mayer, Davis
and Schoorman (1995), who describe trust as the \"willingness of a party
to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the
expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to
the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that
other party\" (p. 712). Research on automation transferred this
understanding to technical systems but defined trust as an attitude
rather than a willingness to act (Lee & See, 2004, p. 51), and
Gillespie, Lockey, Curtis, Pool and Akbari (2023, p. 12) describe trust
in AI as based on positive expectations of how a system will operate.
These definitions share three elements: an expectation about the other
party, a situation of uncertainty and the vulnerability of the person
who trusts.

Three distinctions are necessary to use the construct precisely. First,
trust must be separated from trustworthiness: ability, benevolence and
integrity are characteristics of the trustee that make it appear
trustworthy, whereas trust itself resides in the trustor (Mayer et al.,
1995, p. 717). Second, trust differs from reliance. Trust is an attitude
and reliance is a behavior (Lee & See, 2004, p. 53), so reliance is
treated here as a possible consequence of trust, not as part of it.
Third, trust in a specific system differs from the general propensity to
trust, a stable characteristic of the trustor (Mayer et al., 1995, p.
715). Hoff and Bashir (2015, p. 413) accordingly distinguish
dispositional trust from learned trust, which is based on past
experience with a specific system. This thesis therefore refers to the
AI tools employees have used in their work, so that the trust captured
relates to these tools rather than to a general disposition.

Trust in technology rests on different beliefs than trust in people,
because a technology lacks volition and moral agency (McKnight, Carter,
Thatcher & Clay, 2011, p. 12:5). McKnight et al. (2011, p. 12:9)
therefore replace the interpersonal beliefs of ability, benevolence and
integrity with beliefs about functionality, helpfulness and reliability.
Choung, David and Ross (2022, p. 4) make a comparable distinction for AI
between trust in its functionality and trust in its human-like
qualities, and in their study functionality-related trust had the
stronger total effect on the intention to use AI (Choung et al., 2022,
p. 9). Because the workplace use of AI examined here is task-oriented,
this thesis focuses on functionality-related trust. Trust in AI is
understood as an employee\'s positive expectation that the AI tools they
use at work are functional, competent and reliable (see Choung et al.,
2022, p. 7).

Among the user-related antecedents of trust, competence-related beliefs
play a notable role. A review of 562 studies reports robust positive
effects of familiarity with AI, technical self-efficacy and perceived
mastery on trust in AI (Dang & Li, 2026, p. 678). Section 3.2 develops
this link for AI self-efficacy.

More trust is not automatically better. Lee and See (2004, p. 55) define
calibration as the correspondence between a person\'s trust and the
actual capabilities of a system; overtrust can lead to misuse and
distrust to disuse. Trust in AI is therefore not interpreted here as
uncritical dependence but as a belief in the reliability of a tool that
remains open to correction.

Finally, trust is one of the more consistent predictors of AI
acceptance. A systematic review found that trust, together with
perceived usefulness, performance expectancy, attitudes and effort
expectancy, positively predicted behavioral intention, willingness to
use and use behavior of AI across multiple industries (Kelly et al.,
2023, p. 30). A global survey similarly identified trust as a key driver
of AI acceptance (Gillespie et al., 2023, p. 62), and Dang and Li (2026,
p. 678) conclude that trust in AI drives behavioral intentions across a
wide range of applications. Trust thus forms the first of the two
mediating mechanisms in the research model; Section 3.4 develops its
link to behavioral intention.

### 2.6 AI-Induced Job Insecurity

Trust describes how employees evaluate an AI tool as a means of getting
their work done. A second evaluation concerns not the tool but the
employee\'s own position. AI systems increasingly perform tasks such as
writing, decision-making or creative work that were previously regarded
as the domain of humans, so that workers may see AI both as a tool and
as a potential competitor (Oksanen & Savolainen, 2026, p. 41). Survey
data indicate that about three in five workers are worried about losing
their jobs entirely to AI within the next ten years, particularly those
who actually work with AI (OECD, 2023, p. 5). A multilevel review
accordingly notes that the effects of employees\' awareness that AI may
replace them are transmitted, among other things, through increased
perceptions of job insecurity (Bankins et al., 2024, p. 166). To specify
this construct precisely, it is first traced back to the general concept
of job insecurity.

Greenhalgh and Rosenblatt (1984) define job insecurity as \"perceived
powerlessness to maintain desired continuity in a threatened job
situation\" (p. 438). Job insecurity is thus subjective: it reflects the
anticipation of an involuntary event, and two employees in the same
situation can experience different degrees of insecurity because they
interpret the situation differently (Sverke, Hellgren & Näswall, 2002,
p. 243). Shoss (2017) synthesizes this tradition and defines job
insecurity as \"a perceived threat to the continuity and stability of
employment as it is currently experienced\" (p. 1914). She distinguishes
quantitative job insecurity, which refers to threats to the job as a
whole, from qualitative job insecurity, which refers to threats to
features of the job (Shoss, 2017, p. 1914).

A second distinction concerns the cognitive and the affective side of
the construct. Jiang and Lavaysse (2018, p. 2308) define cognitive job
insecurity as the perceived threat to the continuity of one\'s
employment or to features of the job, and affective job insecurity as
the emotional reactions to this threat, such as concern, worry or fear.
Their meta-analysis shows that the two are distinct, that affective job
insecurity has stronger relationships with most outcomes and that it
mediates the relationships between cognitive job insecurity and most of
its outcomes (Jiang & Lavaysse, 2018, p. 2329). The perceived likelihood
of losing one\'s job is therefore the cognitive appraisal at the
beginning of this process, whereas worry and anxiety are affective
reactions that may follow from it. Focusing on the cognitive form is a
conservative choice with regard to effect sizes, but it is the form that
corresponds to the appraisal of threat described in Section 2.3.

Research on technology has applied these concepts to automation.
Brougham and Haar (2018, p. 241) introduced STARA awareness, the extent
to which employees view smart technology, AI, robotics and algorithms as
likely to affect their future career prospects. Building on this work,
Walczok and Bipp (2025, p. 6) define cognitive automation-related job
insecurity as employees\' perceived possibility of task and job
substitution by these technologies and affective automation-related job
insecurity as their concern, worry and anxiety about such substitution.
Affective automation-related job insecurity was only weakly related to
general job insecurity, and it was positively rather than negatively
related to the use of these technologies (Walczok & Bipp, 2025, p. 8).
AI-related job insecurity is therefore a specific form of job insecurity
whose source is a particular technology rather than the economic
situation of the organization, and experience with AI does not
automatically reduce it.

Against this background, the construct of the present study can be
specified. AI-induced job insecurity is understood as an employee\'s
perceived likelihood of losing their current job because of the
introduction of AI. The definition is cognitive and quantitative: it
refers to the expected loss of the job as a whole, not to the emotional
reaction to it and not to the deterioration of individual job features.
This specification follows the measurement used in this thesis (Kim &
Kim, 2024, p. 8; see Section 4.2), whose items capture the perceived
threat of replacement but not skill obsolescence or changes to one\'s
professional role. The broader title term perceived job threat is
therefore operationalized in this thesis as cognitive AI-induced job
insecurity.

The construct must also be kept apart from AI anxiety, which Wang and
Wang (2019) define as \"an overall, affective response of anxiety or
fear that inhibits an individual from interacting with AI\" (p. 3).
Their scale contains a job replacement factor, but this factor refers to
the fear that AI may replace human jobs in general (Wang & Wang, 2019,
p. 8), not to the perceived likelihood of losing one\'s own job.
Organizational research accordingly treats AI-related job insecurity as
a cognitive stressor and AI use anxiety as the emotional response that
arises when employees work with AI tools (Huvanandana &
Charoensukmongkol, 2026, p. 194).

Job insecurity has well-documented detrimental consequences for
employees\' job attitudes, organizational attitudes and health (Sverke
et al., 2002, p. 252). How AI-related job insecurity affects behavior
toward AI itself is less clear, because it may hold employees back from
using AI or push them to engage with it. In the research model of this
thesis, it is treated as the second mechanism linking AI self-efficacy
to behavioral intention; Section 3.4 derives the direction of this
relationship.

### 2.7 The Austrian Organizational Context

Austria serves as the empirical setting of this study. It is not treated
as a theoretical construct, but its characteristics explain why the
individual-level mechanisms discussed in Sections 2.3 to 2.6 are
relevant there.

At the organizational level, AI adoption in Austria has accelerated
markedly. The share of enterprises with at least ten employees that use
AI technologies almost doubled from just under 11% in 2023 to 20% in
2024 (Statistik Austria, 2024, p. 1), which places Austria well above
the EU average of 13.48% (European Commission, 2025, p. 11).

At the individual level, the picture is more cautious. In the population
aged 16 to 74, only 5% report having a great deal of knowledge about AI
and a further 22% report having much knowledge (Statistik Austria, 2025,
p. 11), and only 31% have ever used a generative AI tool (Statistik
Austria, 2025, p. 23). Among employed persons, only around 40% consider
AI potentially useful for their own job (Statistik Austria, 2025, p.
35), and 13% are very or rather worried that AI could lead to the loss
of their job (Statistik Austria, 2025, p. 21). In an interview study
with 22 experts from Austria and the Czech Republic, a lack of trust in
AI and a lack of knowledge emerged as two of the four main barriers to
AI implementation in small and medium-sized enterprises (Zavodna,
Überwimmer & Frankus, 2024, pp. 337, 341).

These figures refer to enterprises, to the general population and to
employed persons as a whole, and they cannot be transferred directly to
the employees examined in this study. They suggest, however, a gap
between organizational adoption and individual experience: organizations
are introducing AI quickly, while many people in Austria still have
limited experience with AI and rate their knowledge of it as low, and
many employed persons are not convinced of its usefulness for their own
work. This makes the employee-level factors examined in this study,
namely AI self-efficacy, trust in AI and AI-induced job insecurity, a
relevant lens for understanding whether the organizational adoption of
AI translates into employees\' intention to use it.

## 3 Research Model and Hypotheses

### 3.1 Research Model

Chapter 2 has argued that whether employees use AI depends on how they
evaluate it, that these evaluations can point in opposite directions,
and that social cognitive theory explains both directions through the
same belief. This chapter turns these arguments into a research model
and derives the hypotheses. The model addresses the research question of
this thesis: How does AI self-efficacy influence employees\' behavioral
intention to use AI in Austrian organizations, and to what extent is
this relationship mediated by trust in AI and AI-induced job insecurity?

Figure 1 shows the model. AI self-efficacy is expected to increase trust
in AI (H1) and to reduce AI-induced job insecurity (H2). Trust in AI is
expected to increase behavioral intention (H3), whereas AI-induced job
insecurity is expected to reduce it (H4). Together, these paths form two
indirect effects of AI self-efficacy on behavioral intention, one
through each mediator (H5a, H5b). A direct path from AI self-efficacy to
behavioral intention is also estimated. The hypotheses are stated as
relationships, because the cross-sectional design can show whether the
data are consistent with these directions but cannot establish them (see
Section 4.1). \[Abbildung 1: Forschungsmodell einfügen\]

The model places AI self-efficacy at the start of the process. Much
existing research instead treats self-efficacy as a moderator that
weakens the negative effects of AI on employees (Kim & Kim, 2024, p. 6;
Cao, Yao, Sun, Song & Zhang, 2025, p. 14; Liu & Ye, 2026, p. 6). Social
cognitive theory provides the theoretical basis for modeling it as an
antecedent instead: efficacy beliefs influence not only how people cope
with a threat once it has been perceived but whether a situation is
perceived as threatening in the first place, and they shape how actively
people engage with a new task (see Section 2.3). Two studies have
specified models in this order. Chou, Hsiao, Lu and Su (2026, p. 1476)
proposed a model of health-care workers\' intention to use AI that is
grounded in social cognitive theory and centered on AI self-efficacy,
and Montag, Kraus, Baumann and Rozgonjuk (2023, pp. 3--4) placed
technology self-efficacy before the propensity to trust automated
technology, which mediated its links with the acceptance and fear of AI.

The two mediators correspond to the two functions of self-efficacy
described in Section 2.3. Trust in AI represents the enabling side: it
captures how employees evaluate AI as a tool for their work. AI-induced
job insecurity represents the inhibiting side: it captures how employees
evaluate what AI means for their own job. The following sections derive
each path in turn.

### 3.2 AI Self-Efficacy and Trust in AI

Employees with higher AI self-efficacy are expected to trust AI more.
The argument rests on the link between self-efficacy and engagement.
People get involved in activities they judge themselves capable of
handling and invest more effort and persistence in them (Bandura, 1977,
p. 194; see Section 2.3). Employees who believe they can learn and use
AI effectively are therefore more likely to try AI tools, explore what
they can do and keep using them after initial difficulties. In doing so,
they gain the experience on which judgments of whether an AI tool works
well, is competent and is reliable are built (see Section 2.5). Zhang
and Yu (2025, p. 2) make a similar argument: when users are confident in
their ability to operate and understand AI systems, they are more likely
to recognize the reliability of the system\'s output. Research on trust
in automation also lists self-confidence among the internal factors that
alter situational trust (Hoff & Bashir, 2015, p. 417).

Beyond the review evidence reported in Section 2.5, studies with
AI-specific measures point in the same direction. Among 353 Chinese
university students, generative AI self-efficacy was strongly and
positively related to trust in generative AI (β = .66; Zhang & Yu, 2025,
p. 4). In a German sample of 289 students and members of the general
population, technology self-efficacy also correlated positively with the
propensity to trust automated technology, although this refers to a
general disposition rather than to trust in specific tools (Montag et
al., 2023, pp. 2--3). No study that tested this relationship among
employees with a learning-oriented measure of AI self-efficacy was
identified in the literature reviewed for this thesis.

The relationship is not necessarily simple. In automation research, the
operator\'s self-confidence influences reliance on automation alongside
trust (Lee & See, 2004, p. 53), and among knowledge workers, higher
confidence in their own ability to perform a task went along with more
critical thinking when using generative AI, whereas higher confidence in
generative AI went along with less (Lee et al., 2025, p. 12).
Self-efficacious employees may therefore trust AI more, but not blindly,
which is consistent with the notion of calibrated trust discussed in
Section 2.5. Because trust in AI is defined in this thesis as the
positive expectation that the AI tools one uses at work are functional,
competent and reliable, a positive relationship is nevertheless
expected:

**H1: AI self-efficacy is positively related to trust in AI.**

### 3.3 AI Self-Efficacy and AI-Induced Job Insecurity

Employees with higher AI self-efficacy are expected to perceive less
AI-induced job insecurity. For Bandura (1997, p. 140), threat is
relational: it depends on the match between a person\'s perceived coping
capabilities and the potentially harmful aspects of the environment (see
Section 2.3). An employee who believes they can learn new AI
technologies and use them effectively in their work is likely to see
themselves as someone who can adapt to AI rather than be replaced by it.
Zang, Shao and Li (2024, p. 7) argue in the same direction: employees
with high self-efficacy are \"more likely to appraise AI usage as a
challenge\", while those with low self-efficacy \"constantly imagine
various risks and threats related to AI usage\".

The empirical evidence is consistent for related constructs. Among 210
hospital workers in Taiwan, AI self-efficacy was negatively related to
AI anxiety (β = −.249; Chou et al., 2026, p. 1484), a construct that
includes fear of job replacement (Chou et al., 2026, p. 1479). Among 222
employees of a Chinese technology company, AI self-efficacy correlated
negatively with the appraisal of AI as a threat (r = −.25; Cao et al.,
2025, p. 11). Walczok and Bipp (2025, p. 10) found that core
self-evaluations, a broad personality trait that includes self-efficacy
(Walczok & Bipp, 2025, p. 4), negatively predicted the initial level of
affective automation-related job insecurity (β = −.136).

For job insecurity itself, the evidence is weaker. Kim and Kim (2024, p.
9), whose AI-induced job insecurity scale this thesis adapts, report a
correlation of zero between self-efficacy in AI learning and AI-induced
job insecurity (r = .00). Liu and Ye (2026, p. 6) found a non-linear
pattern: the positive relationship between AI application and job
insecurity was strongest at moderate levels of self-efficacy and weaker
at low and high levels. They explain this by suggesting that employees
with moderate self-efficacy know enough to recognize the threat but lack
the confidence to feel secure (Liu & Ye, 2026, p. 6). Self-efficacy
therefore appears to be associated with a weaker appraisal of AI as a
threat, while its relationship with the cognitive judgment that one\'s
job may be lost is not yet established. H2 follows the theoretical
argument, but it is the hypothesis with the least empirical support:

**H2: AI self-efficacy is negatively related to AI-induced job
insecurity.**

### 3.4 Trust in AI, AI-Induced Job Insecurity and Behavioral Intention

Trust in AI is expected to increase employees\' intention to use it.
According to Lee and See (2004, p. 52), trust guides reliance on
automation especially when its complexity makes a complete understanding
impractical. This applies to AI, whose outputs are difficult to trace
(see Section 2.5). An employee who intends to use AI in their work
therefore commits to relying on a system they cannot fully check, and
trust makes this commitment easier. Section 2.5 has shown that trust is
one of the more consistent predictors of AI acceptance across reviews
and surveys. In the study by Zhang and Yu (2025, p. 4), trust in
generative AI also predicted acceptance when generative AI self-efficacy
was included in the model, although the effect was small (β = .15).

**H3: Trust in AI is positively related to behavioral intention to use
AI.**

AI-induced job insecurity is expected to reduce behavioral intention.
Social cognitive theory holds that people tend to avoid situations they
believe exceed their coping skills (Bandura, 1977, p. 194). For an
employee who fears being replaced by AI, using AI means engaging with
the very technology that threatens their job. A multilevel review
accordingly notes that fear-based attitudes toward AI can impede
employees\' adoption of AI technologies (Bankins et al., 2024, p. 166).
Besides the finding by Vu and Lim reported in Section 2.2, the empirical
evidence is mostly indirect. AI anxiety was negatively related to
health-care workers\' intention to use an AI system (β = −.102; Chou et
al., 2026, p. 1484), and among 391 hotel employees in Turkey,
qualitative job insecurity, that is, the fear of losing valued features
of one\'s job, was negatively related to perceived usefulness, perceived
ease of use and usage behavior (Çalışkan, Sökmen, Sökmen & Arıcı, 2026,
pp. 3133, 3137).

Other studies point in the opposite direction. In a study by Sha and
Chai (2025, p. 5), job insecurity mediated a positive relationship
between digital-AI transformation and job crafting. Among 560 nurses,
job insecurity mediated a positive relationship between perceived AI
substitution and active learning behavior (Zheng, Xu, Yin, Lin, Liang &
Du, 2026, pp. 3--4). Among 421 adults in Turkey, fear of job
displacement was the factor most strongly related to dependency on AI (β
= .474; Dursun, 2026, pp. 6, 14). These outcomes differ from the one
examined here: learning, job crafting and dependency are ways of coping
with a threat, whereas behavioral intention captures the voluntary
decision to use AI in one\'s work. They show, however, that threatened
employees may also turn toward AI rather than away from it. H4 states
the negative direction suggested by social cognitive theory and
acceptance research, and the alternative is taken up again in the
discussion:

**H4: AI-induced job insecurity is negatively related to behavioral
intention to use AI.**

### 3.5 The Mediating Roles of Trust in AI and AI-Induced Job Insecurity

H1 to H4 together imply that AI self-efficacy is related to behavioral
intention indirectly. Through the enabling path, higher AI self-efficacy
is expected to increase trust in AI, which in turn increases behavioral
intention. Through the inhibiting path, higher AI self-efficacy is
expected to reduce AI-induced job insecurity; because job insecurity is
expected to lower behavioral intention, less of it means a higher
intention. Both indirect effects are therefore expected to be positive.

Prior findings on this indirect logic are mixed. Chou et al. (2026, p.
1485) found no significant direct effect of AI self-efficacy on
health-care workers\' intention to use AI (β = .037), whereas its
effects through outcome expectations, affect and AI anxiety were
significant. Zhang and Yu (2025, p. 4), in contrast, found a strong
direct effect (β = .49) alongside a small indirect effect through trust
(0.10, 95% CI \[0.004, 0.19\]), so that the indirect effect through
trust accounted for only a small part of the total effect. Trust and job
insecurity are therefore unlikely to capture everything that AI
self-efficacy contributes, and the model retains the direct path from AI
self-efficacy to behavioral intention. For the path through job
insecurity, no study that tested an indirect effect of AI self-efficacy
on behavioral intention via AI-induced job insecurity was identified in
the literature reviewed; H5b therefore rests on the combination of H2
and H4. Whether and to what extent the effect is transmitted through the
two mediators is not hypothesized but determined empirically (see
Section 4.4).

The two mediators are modeled in parallel rather than in sequence. Job
insecurity is strongly and negatively related to employees\' trust in
their organization (r̄c = −.498; Sverke et al., 2002, p. 249), so a link
between the two mediators is conceivable. That finding, however,
concerns trust in the employer and not trust in an AI tool, and the
literature reviewed for this thesis provides no theoretical basis for
deciding whether AI-induced job insecurity precedes trust in AI or
follows from it. A sequential model would therefore rest on an untested
assumption. The parallel model avoids this and allows the two specific
indirect effects to be compared directly. This comparison is a central
contribution of the study: it indicates whether the association between
AI self-efficacy and behavioral intention runs mainly through trust in
AI or through lower AI-induced job insecurity.

Accordingly, trust in AI and AI-induced job insecurity are expected to
mediate the relationship between AI self-efficacy and behavioral
intention to use AI in parallel:

**H5a: AI self-efficacy has a positive indirect effect on behavioral
intention through trust in AI.**

**H5b: AI self-efficacy has a positive indirect effect on behavioral
intention through AI-induced job insecurity.**

are mixed. No study in the literature reviewed for this thesis has

## 4 Methodology

### 4.1 Research Design and Data Collection

Chapter 3 derived a model with four constructs and six hypotheses, all
specified before any data are collected. Testing such a model requires a
design that measures the constructs in a standardized way and estimates
the strength and direction of the relationships between them. This
thesis therefore uses a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design. The
constructs are treated as measurable phenomena, which corresponds to a
positivist research orientation. A qualitative design could provide
richer insight into how employees experience AI, but it could not test a
parallel mediation model with several latent constructs.

The data are collected through a standardized online questionnaire.
Primary data are necessary because the constructs refer to each
respondent\'s own work situation: whether an employee feels capable of
learning AI, trusts the AI tools at hand or fears being replaced by them
depends on the tools and the organization they work in, and no existing
dataset captures these perceptions together. Because AI self-efficacy,
trust in AI, AI-induced job insecurity and behavioral intention cannot
be observed directly, each is measured with an established multi-item
scale and modeled as a reflective latent construct. The model is
estimated with partial least squares structural equation modeling
(PLS-SEM), which is justified in Section 4.4.

Because all constructs are measured at one point in time, the analysis
can show whether the relationships are consistent with the hypothesized
model, but it cannot establish causality; Section 4.5 returns to this
point.

### 4.2 Construct Operationalization

All four constructs are measured with scales taken from prior research
and adapted to the work context. The scales were selected primarily for
how closely they match the definitions developed in Chapter 2. Each
scale keeps its original response format, so that the scales are changed
as little as possible: AI self-efficacy and behavioral intention are
rated on a seven-point Likert scale, trust in AI and AI-induced job
insecurity on a five-point Likert scale, each ranging from \"strongly
disagree\" to \"strongly agree\". The different formats also serve as a
procedural remedy against common method bias (Section 4.5). Table 2
summarizes the instruments; the full item list in English and German is
provided in Appendix \[X\].

AI self-efficacy is measured with the AI learning readiness
self-efficacy scale (AILRSE-5) by Oksanen et al. (2026). The five items
begin with \"I\'m confident in my ability to ...\" and refer to
understanding how new AI technologies work and to learning how to use
them if necessary, in daily life, to solve a problem and independently
(Oksanen et al., 2026, p. 4). The scale was validated with 20,173
respondents in 12 countries and showed high internal consistency in all
samples, with Cronbach\'s α and McDonald\'s ω of at least .94 (Oksanen
et al., 2026, pp. 3, 5). It is also the only one of the three
operationalizations discussed in Section 2.4 that has been administered
in German: 736 respondents in Germany answered a back-translated version
(Oksanen et al., 2026, pp. 3--4), with α = .96 and an average variance
extracted (AVE) of .851 (Oksanen et al., 2026, p. 5).

Two items are adapted to the work context, in line with the definition
in Section 2.4: \"in daily life\" is replaced by \"in my daily work\",
and \"to solve a problem\" by \"to solve a work-related problem\".
Because of this adaptation, the validation results of the German version
cannot be transferred directly; the reliability and validity of the
adapted scale are therefore examined in the pre-test and in the
measurement model. AILRSE-5 replaces the four-item scale by Kim and Kim
(2024) planned in the proposal, for the reason given in Section 2.4 and
because no German version of that scale is available. \[Annahme prüfen:
Begründung mit Betreuer abstimmen\]

Trust in AI is measured with the functionality trust scale by Choung et
al. (2022). It corresponds to the definition in Section 2.5, the
positive expectation that the AI tools one uses at work are functional,
competent and reliable. The five items state that the technologies
\"work well\", \"have the features necessary to complete key tasks\",
\"are competent in their area of expertise\", \"are reliable\" and \"are
dependable\"; in the original study they were rated on a five-point
scale and reached α = .90 and α = .91 in two samples (Choung et al.,
2022, p. 13). The original referent \"smart technologies\" is replaced
by \"the AI tools I have used in my work\". This anchors the trust
judgment in the respondent\'s own tools rather than in AI in general,
which matches the understanding of learned trust described in Section
2.5.

AI-induced job insecurity is measured with the five-item scale by Kim
and Kim (2024), who adapted the job security scale used by Kraimer,
Wayne, Liden and Sparrowe (2005, p. 392) to the context of AI
implementation (Kim & Kim, 2024, p. 7). The adapted items refer to the
perceived likelihood of losing one\'s job because of AI, for example
\"The introduction of artificial intelligence technologies has made my
job at work insecure\" and \"Even if I want to, I will not be able to
keep my present job due to the adoption of artificial intelligence\"
(Kim & Kim, 2024, p. 8). The scale thus captures the cognitive,
quantitative form of job insecurity defined in Section 2.6. In the
original study it was rated on a five-point scale (Kim & Kim, 2024, p.
7) and reached α = .954 (Kim & Kim, 2024, p. 8).

Behavioral intention is measured with the three items from UTAUT: \"I
intend to use the system in the next \<n\> months\", \"I predict I would
use the system in the next \<n\> months\" and \"I plan to use the system
in the next \<n\> months\" (Venkatesh et al., 2003, p. 460). \"The
system\" is replaced by \"AI tools in my work\", and the time frame is
set to six months. In line with Section 2.2, the items thus capture the
intention to use AI at work in the coming months, which may mean
continued, renewed or expanded use. In the original study, the scale
reached an internal consistency reliability of .92 at the first
measurement (Venkatesh et al., 2003, p. 458).

The constructs deliberately refer to different objects. AI self-efficacy
refers to new AI technologies in general, because it captures the belief
that one can learn technologies one may not yet use. AI-induced job
insecurity refers to AI as a whole, because a job can be threatened by
AI applications that the respondent does not use personally. Trust in
AI, in contrast, refers to the AI tools the respondent has used at work,
in line with the understanding of trust in a specific technology
(McKnight et al., 2011, p. 12:7), and behavioral intention refers to AI
tools in the respondent\'s work. In addition, respondents are asked
which AI tool they use most often for their work; this information is
used only to describe the sample.

The questionnaire also records age, gender, educational level,
occupational field, industry, years of work experience and the current
frequency of AI use. Age, gender and educational level enter the
structural model as control variables on behavioral intention, because
in the Austrian population, younger, more highly educated and male
persons use AI more often and hold more positive attitudes toward it
(Statistik Austria, 2025, p. 35). Frequency of AI use is not included in
the main model, because it is related to AI self-efficacy in both
directions (see Section 4.5) and closely tied to the intention to
continue using AI, so that controlling for it would absorb part of the
effects under study. It is used only in a robustness check (Section
4.4). The remaining characteristics are used to describe the sample.

### 4.3 Sampling Strategy and Survey Procedure

The target population consists of employees in Austrian organizations
who have used AI-based tools for their work at least once. A filter
question at the start of the questionnaire asks whether the respondent
has ever used AI-based tools for their current job, such as ChatGPT,
Microsoft Copilot, AI-supported decision tools or automated workflows.
Respondents who have never done so are excluded, because they cannot
evaluate AI tools from their own work experience. The criterion is
deliberately broader than regular use: restricting the sample to regular
users would exclude those employees who have tried AI but use it rarely
or no longer, for example because they feel uncertain or threatened, and
would thus restrict the variance of the constructs under study. How
often respondents currently use AI is recorded separately (not in the
last six months, less than monthly, monthly, weekly, daily).

The study uses non-probability sampling. Participants are recruited
through convenience sampling and targeted outreach via LinkedIn and
direct contacts in organizations, aiming at employees from different
sectors such as finance, professional services, manufacturing and
healthcare. The questionnaire takes about ten to twelve minutes and
remains open for four weeks. Because the sample is not random, the
results cannot be generalized to all employees in Austria. The
restriction to AI users also has a substantive consequence. Because only
a minority of the Austrian population has used generative AI (Section
2.7), AI users are likely to be a more experienced subgroup. This is
appropriate for the research question, but it should be considered when
the level of AI self-efficacy and trust in the sample is interpreted.

The target is at least 200 valid responses after data screening, ideally
250. In PLS-SEM, the required sample size depends on the model
structure, the expected effect sizes and the desired statistical power
rather than on a fixed threshold (Hair, Risher, Sarstedt & Ringle, 2019,
p. \[S. prüfen\]). Hair et al. (2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]) refer to the
inverse square root method by Kock and Hadaya (2018) for estimating the
minimum sample size. Kock and Hadaya (2018, p. 235) derive the constant
of this method from a one-tailed test; because the hypotheses in this
study are tested with two-sided confidence intervals (Section 4.4), the
corresponding two-tailed form is used. For a significance level of 5%
and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum sample size is then ((1.96 +
0.842) / \|β_min\|)², where β_min is the smallest path coefficient
expected to be significant. With 200 respondents, path coefficients of
about .20 and larger can be detected; with 250 respondents, coefficients
of about .18. Smaller effects, such as β = .15 for trust on acceptance
(Zhang & Yu, 2025, p. 4) or β = −.10 for AI anxiety on intention (Chou
et al., 2026, p. 1484), would require about 350 and 790 respondents. An
indirect effect is the product of two standardized paths that are each
smaller than one in absolute value, so it is smaller than either path
and harder to detect. A non-significant result, particularly for H4 and
H5b, can therefore not be interpreted as the absence of an effect.

Low variance in AI-induced job insecurity is a further risk. Among
employed persons in Austria, 13% are very or rather worried that AI
could lead to the loss of their job, whereas 48% are not worried at all
(Statistik Austria, 2025, p. 21). In a German-language sample, Walczok
and Bipp (2025, p. 6) found a mean of 1.83 on a five-point scale for
awareness of job threats from smart technology, AI, robotics and
algorithms. Many respondents are therefore likely to choose the lowest
response options. PLS-SEM does not require normally distributed data,
but a skewed distribution with little variance can weaken the estimated
relationships. The distribution of AI-induced job insecurity is
therefore examined before the structural model is interpreted.

The questionnaire is administered in German. For AI self-efficacy, the
German version used by Oksanen et al. (2026, p. 3) is requested from the
authors and adapted to the work context. \[Annahme prüfen: deutsche
Fassung anfragen\] The other scales are translated into German and
translated back into English by a second person, following the procedure
Walczok and Bipp (2025, p. 5) used for a German-language job insecurity
study. Differences between the back-translation and the original are
resolved before the questionnaire is finalized. Particular attention is
paid to the trust items \"reliable\" and \"dependable\", which are both
commonly translated as \"zuverlässig\" and therefore need two distinct
German terms. Before data collection, the questionnaire is pre-tested
with about five employees who use AI at work, who are asked whether the
items are clear and whether the questionnaire refers to the AI tools
they actually use. \[Annahme prüfen: Umfang des Pretests\] The
questionnaire is implemented in \[Umfragetool einfügen\].

### 4.4 Data Analysis

The model is estimated with PLS-SEM in SmartPLS \[Version einfügen\].
PLS-SEM is a causal-predictive approach that estimates models designed
to provide causal explanations while also emphasizing prediction (Hair
et al., 2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]). The term refers to the estimation logic
of the method; it does not imply that cross-sectional data can establish
causality. It is suitable for this study for three reasons. First, the
model contains four latent constructs, two parallel mediators and
several indirect effects that are estimated simultaneously. Second, the
study extends established acceptance research with AI-specific
constructs, which Hair et al. (2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]) list as a typical
application. Third, it is expected that AI-induced job insecurity will
not be normally distributed (Section 4.3). Non-normal data alone,
however, is not a sufficient reason to choose PLS-SEM (Hair et al.,
2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]); it is an additional advantage alongside the
first two reasons.

The analysis follows two steps. First, the reflective measurement models
are assessed. Indicator loadings should exceed .708, which means that
the construct explains more than half of the indicator\'s variance (Hair
et al., 2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]). Internal consistency reliability is
assessed with Cronbach\'s α and composite reliability; values between
.70 and .90 are satisfactory to good, whereas values of .95 and above
indicate redundant items (Hair et al., 2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]). This
upper limit is relevant here, because AILRSE-5 reached α = .96 in the
German sample (Oksanen et al., 2026, p. 5). A value above .95 would
therefore be reported and discussed rather than treated as a sign of
particular quality. Convergent validity requires an AVE of at least .50.
Discriminant validity is assessed with the heterotrait-monotrait ratio
(HTMT); because the four constructs are conceptually distinct, the
stricter threshold of .85 applies (Hair et al., 2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]),
and bootstrap confidence intervals are used to test whether the HTMT
values are significantly below it.

Second, the structural model is assessed. Collinearity between predictor
constructs is checked with the variance inflation factor (VIF); values
of 5 and above indicate collinearity problems, and values should ideally
be 3 or lower (Hair et al., 2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]). The path
coefficients for H1 to H4 are tested for significance with bootstrapping
based on 5,000 subsamples, using percentile confidence intervals (Hair
et al., 2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]). Explanatory power is assessed with R²,
where values of .75, .50 and .25 are considered substantial, moderate
and weak (Hair et al., 2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]), and predictive power is
assessed with PLSpredict (Hair et al., 2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]).

H5a and H5b are tested through the specific indirect effects of AI
self-efficacy on behavioral intention through trust in AI and through
AI-induced job insecurity (Hair et al., 2019, p. \[S. prüfen\]). An
indirect effect is considered significant if its 95% percentile
bootstrap confidence interval, based on the same 5,000 subsamples, does
not include zero. For H5b, the indirect effect is the product of two
expected negative paths and is therefore expected to be positive. The
type of mediation is then classified following Zhao, Lynch and Chen
(2010, p. 200) on the basis of the indirect and the direct effect: if
only the indirect effect is significant, the mediation is indirect-only;
if both are significant and have the same sign, it is complementary; if
both are significant but have opposite signs, it is competitive; if only
the direct effect is significant, there is no mediation (direct-only);
and if neither is significant, there is no effect.

To address the comparison raised in Section 3.5, the difference between
the two specific indirect effects is tested with a bootstrap confidence
interval. If this interval does not include zero, one mechanism carries
significantly more of the effect of AI self-efficacy than the other.
Because the parallel model does not specify a link between the two
mediators, their correlation is reported alongside the results. In
addition, an exploratory comparison examines whether employees who
currently use AI at least monthly differ from those who use it less
often or have not used it in the last six months in their mean scores of
AI self-efficacy, trust in AI, AI-induced job insecurity and behavioral
intention. This comparison is not hypothesized and is reported as a
supplementary analysis; if one of the groups is very small, it is
reported descriptively only. \[Annahme prüfen: Testverfahren für den
Gruppenvergleich mit Betreuer abstimmen\] Finally, as a robustness
check, the structural model is re-estimated with frequency of AI use as
an additional control variable on all three endogenous constructs.

### 4.5 Quality Criteria and Ethical Considerations

Because all constructs are measured through self-reports by the same
respondents at one point in time, common method bias is the main threat
to validity. Relationships between constructs may then partly reflect
the shared measurement method, for example through a respondent\'s
tendency to answer consistently, rather than the theoretical
relationships alone (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee & Podsakoff, 2003, p.
882). When predictor and criterion cannot be measured at different times
or from different sources, procedural and statistical remedies should be
combined (Podsakoff et al., 2003, p. 898). Several procedural remedies
are applied: respondents are assured that their answers are anonymous
and that there are no right or wrong answers, which reduces evaluation
apprehension; predictor and criterion variables are placed in separate
sections of the questionnaire; items are worded clearly, and the
pre-test is used to remove ambiguous wording; and the mediators are
rated in a different response format than the predictor and the
criterion (Section 4.2) (Podsakoff et al., 2003, pp. 887--888).
Statistical checks serve only as diagnostics and cannot rule out common
method bias. Harman\'s single-factor test is reported because it is
widely used, although it only indicates whether a single factor accounts
for most of the covariance and does not control for method effects
(Podsakoff et al., 2003, p. 889). It is complemented by a full
collinearity assessment, in which a variance inflation factor above 3.3
for any latent variable indicates possible contamination by common
method bias (Kock, 2015, p. 6). The procedural remedies therefore remain
the main safeguard.

The cross-sectional design also limits causal interpretation. The model
places AI self-efficacy at the start, but the reverse direction is
plausible: using AI can strengthen AI self-efficacy (Li & Geng, 2026, p.
14; Oksanen et al., 2026, p. 6), and positive experiences with AI could
increase both self-efficacy and trust. The robustness check with
frequency of AI use (Section 4.4) shows how sensitive the results are to
this variable, but it cannot resolve the question of direction. The
results are therefore interpreted as consistent or inconsistent with the
hypothesized model, not as proof of causal effects. A longitudinal
design would be needed to test the direction of the relationships.
