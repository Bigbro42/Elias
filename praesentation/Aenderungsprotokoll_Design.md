## Appendix: speaker notes (replace completely)

### Slide 1 – Energy Cluster Norway

Welcome, everyone. We are Group 1, and we looked at Norway’s offshore energy technology cluster and at Energy Cluster Norway, or ECN, which was launched in August this year as the cluster’s new national Institution for Collaboration. Over the next 25 minutes we follow the logic of the course. We start with Norway’s economic performance and business environment. Then we go into the cluster itself: its history, its size, its actors and its Diamond. In the last part we derive a value proposition, three priorities for ECN and recommendations for each group of actors. Our analysis rests on published data and on a written interview with ECN’s CEO, Owe Hagesæther, who allowed us to quote him by name. At the end we are also open about what our analysis cannot show yet.

### Slide 2 – Norway must turn the prosperity it inherited from petroleum into prosperity it creates wit

Our central thesis builds on a distinction from the course. Inherited prosperity comes from natural resources and is limited by them. Created prosperity comes from productivity and innovation. Norway has plenty of the first kind, and petroleum also gave it world-class offshore capabilities: subsea engineering, operator know-how and safety systems. Our research question is therefore: how can ECN help turn these capabilities into globally competitive energy technologies, in floating wind, CO₂ storage and offshore electrification? Our short answer: collaboration is not the problem. Norway already collaborates more than almost any country in Europe. The problem is the step from a successful demonstration to a first commercial order. The presentation has three parts: diagnosis, cluster and strategy.

### Slide 3 – High level, but falling behind on growth

Let’s start with performance. On levels, Norway is outstanding: GDP per capita of around 91,000 dollars in purchasing power terms, and the third-highest output per hour worked in the OECD. Growth tells a different story. Between 2014 and 2024, prosperity grew by only about 0.9 percent a year, roughly half the average of the course sample. In the MOC framework this puts Norway in the “high but declining” quadrant. Two caveats matter. First, the productivity figure includes the very capital-intensive petroleum sector, so it flatters the rest of the economy. Second, petroleum still makes up 57 percent of goods exports. So Norway does not have a level problem. It has a growth and transition problem, and that is increasingly a microeconomic question.

### Slide 4 – Rich and productive, but losing ground on growth

This chart shows the same point in the format of the course exhibits. On the left is prosperity: GDP per capita on the vertical axis and its growth from 2014 to 2024 on the horizontal axis. The dashed lines are the sample averages. Norway is far above the average level, but to the left of the average growth line. On the right, labour productivity shows the same pattern: a very high level of about 180,000 dollars per person employed, but growth of only around 0.8 percent a year, against an average of 1.1. We read both positions from the course charts, so they are approximate. The message is clear, though. Norway sits in the upper-left quadrant twice: rich and productive, but losing ground. Without new sources of productivity growth, this gap will widen as petroleum production matures.

### Slide 5 – Top-three productivity, but petroleum inflates it

How does Norway compare with its natural peers? This chart shows GDP per hour worked in 2025 for selected European OECD countries. Norway is third with about 100 dollars, behind Ireland and Luxembourg, whose figures are pushed up by multinational profits and cross-border commuters. Norway is ahead of Denmark, Sweden, Finland and the Netherlands, which we treat as the most relevant peers. But again, the petroleum sector is included. Oil and gas production has extremely high value added per hour, so the mainland economy is closer to its Nordic neighbours than the chart suggests. And on innovation, which we look at in a moment, these same peers are ahead of Norway. So the productivity lead is real, but a good part of it is inherited rather than created.

### Slide 6 – Strong buffers, weak commercial output

Two quick views complete the performance picture. On the macro side, mainland GDP grew by 1.7 percent in 2025, and about the same is expected this year. Inflation is coming back towards the 2 percent target. Unemployment is around 4 percent; the labour market is tight, and participation has been declining. The fiscal position is exceptional: in 2026 the government spends 2.7 percent of the oil fund, below the 3 percent rule. But the non-oil deficit equals 12.6 percent of mainland trend GDP, so the budget leans heavily on the fund. On the micro side, petroleum still dominates goods exports, while energy suppliers export around 200 billion kroner. Education is a clear strength, but intellectual assets rank only 30th and business R&D is at 65 percent of the EU average. The orange items are the watch points. The macro buffers are strong; the commercial output is weak.

### Slide 7 – Strong in collaboration, weak in commercial results

The European Innovation Scoreboard sharpens this picture. Norway is a “Strong Innovator”, but it ranks only tenth and trails its Nordic peers. What stands out is the shape of the profile. Linkages, meaning collaboration between firms, universities and research institutes, are at 237 percent of the EU average, second in Europe. Tertiary education is also very strong. But the closer we get to the market, the weaker it becomes: business R&D at 65, intellectual assets at 49, new-to-market sales at 48, and medium- and high-tech exports at rank 38 of 40. So the quantity of collaboration is not the problem. Converting it into commercial outcomes is. We call this the commercialisation gap. One caveat: because petroleum dominates exports, the high-tech export share is low almost by definition. We therefore treat these output indicators as a signal and test it with the cluster analysis and two cases later on.

### Slide 8 – Legacy, geography and macro policy

Now the business environment, starting with legacy. Norway inherited a high-trust, egalitarian society, a small population of 5.6 million and a Nordic model in which state, employers and unions cooperate. State ownership is part of that: Equinor is two-thirds state-owned. The resource legacy is hydropower, shipping, fisheries and, since Ekofisk in 1969, oil and gas. Regionally, Norway sits on Europe’s north-western edge, with high costs and a harsh sea, outside the EU but inside the single market through the EEA. That gives market access, but EU state-aid rules also apply, for example to the support for Utsira Nord. Norway is a key gas supplier and now stores CO₂ for the Netherlands, Denmark and Sweden. Macro policy is a strength: an inflation target and a floating krone since 2001, and a fiscal rule that spends only the fund’s expected real return. The real challenge lies at the microeconomic level.

### Slide 9 – Micro policy and the national Diamond

On micro policy, the structural setting is open: EEA market access, and a 78 percent petroleum tax that captures resource rents. Competition is open in form, through licensing rounds, but a few large buyers dominate. Privatisation was partial: Equinor is listed, but the state keeps 67 percent. Education is strong, and SkatteFUNN supports business R&D through the tax system. Cluster policy runs through the Norwegian Innovation Clusters programme, and three regional cluster bodies are now merging into ECN. Demand policy is the key lever: the state funds Longship, supported Hywind Tampen through Enova and is now backing Utsira Nord. In Porter’s national Diamond, factor conditions and related industries are strong, and demand is sophisticated but small. Strategy and rivalry are mixed because ownership is concentrated and private R&D is low. Overall, the environment is rich but narrow, and it is slow to create new industries.

### Slide 10 – Five phases: from discovery to energy transition

Before we look at the cluster today, a quick look at how it developed in five phases. First, the 1960s and 70s: Ekofisk, then Statoil and the Petroleum Directorate in 1972, with local-content rules that let Norwegian firms into the industry. Second, the 1980s and 90s: yards and engineering firms such as Aker and Kværner learned offshore work, and the NORSOK programme cut project costs. Third, the 2000s: subsea suppliers went international, and Statoil was listed in 2001. Fourth, the oil-price fall of 2014 forced a reset. Suppliers cut costs and began to look at offshore wind and CCS, which is where today’s transition really started. Fifth, since 2021: Hywind Tampen, Northern Lights and now ECN. The pattern is that each phase added a capability, and collaboration institutions came last, as in the Australian wine cluster we discussed in class.

### Slide 11 – Offshore energy technology and services

Here is how we drew the cluster boundary. The core is offshore energy technology and services, meaning the firms and institutions that design, build, install, operate and decommission energy systems at sea. That includes oil and gas, but also floating wind, CO₂ storage and electrification, because these applications draw on the same skills, suppliers and customers. Around the core sit specialised suppliers such as Aker Solutions or Kongsberg, a strong knowledge base with NTNU and SINTEF, demanding customers, and related clusters such as maritime and hydropower. One analytical distinction is important for us: the cluster is the whole network. ECN is one Institution for Collaboration within it, next to Offshore Norge, Norwep and others. That is why we later ask what ECN should do that the others do not.

### Slide 12 – A large, export-oriented cluster in transition

How big is this cluster? Norwegian energy suppliers export around 200 billion kroner a year. ECN alone brings together more than 350 member companies. On the demand side the picture is concentrated: the largest buyer, Equinor, is 67 percent state-owned. In the new segments, Northern Lights shows what scale can look like. Its storage capacity is planned to grow from 1.5 to at least 5 million tonnes of CO₂ a year in phase two. [Fill in once the data is in: number of employees in the supplier industry and how it developed since 2014; share of supplier revenue from wind, CCS and electrification.] Together, these figures show a strong export base. The open question is how fast the new segments grow compared with oil and gas, and that is exactly where ECN comes in.

### Slide 13 – One national platform, three equal regions

ECN brings together three regional cluster organisations: GCE Ocean Technology in Bergen, Energy Transition Norway in Stavanger and Energy Valley in Oslo. Each region contributes something different, from subsea deep tech in Bergen to operators and first customers in Stavanger and finance and AI in Oslo. To understand how this works in practice, we contacted ECN’s CEO, Owe Hagesæther. Three points stood out. The model was built bottom-up, after interviews with more than a hundred companies. Governance is deliberately balanced, with one third of the shares and three board seats per region. And services are distributed rather than duplicated: the MIT scale-up programme runs in Bergen and Boston, the Technical Committee in Stavanger serves members nationwide. We noticed that ECN currently measures success by integration milestones and member feedback. That is where our recommendations come in.

### Slide 14 – Strong inputs, weak demand for new technology at scale

Applying the Diamond to the cluster confirms the national picture. Factor conditions are excellent: engineering depth, test sites and research, although specialists are scarce and the grid sets limits. Related and supporting industries are strong and improving, partly thanks to ECN. Strategy and rivalry are mixed: there is strong technical competition among suppliers, but only a few powerful buyers, led by the majority state-owned Equinor, and business R&D is low. Demand is very sophisticated in oil and gas, and cross-border demand for CO₂ storage is growing, but floating wind still depends on state support. These two corners are linked. Because demand for new technology is concentrated in a handful of buyers and state-backed projects, they decide whether a demonstration ever becomes a commercial order. That is the weak link in the Diamond.

### Slide 15 – Strategic issues and policies already in place

This slide brings the strategic issues together with the policies that already exist. Reading across: on demand, the state supports Utsira Nord, Hywind Tampen through Enova, and Longship, but none of these instruments builds a lead customer or a procurement route into the support. On strategy and rivalry, SkatteFUNN rewards R&D spending, not commercial conversion. On factor conditions, research programmes and test sites exist, but they are not coordinated across the three regions. And on related clusters, the Norwegian Innovation Clusters programme and the ECN merger address fragmentation, but there is no common gate process or outcome measurement yet. So the policy toolbox is broad. The gap lies in how the tools are connected to a buyer.

### Slide 16 – Validation alone does not create scale

Two Norwegian flagship projects illustrate this bottleneck. Hywind started as a floating wind demonstrator in 2009. It took more than a decade, and an operator, Equinor, using the power for its own oil fields, before Hywind Tampen went into operation. The next step, Utsira Nord, still depends on a state-aid competition, and its terms were revised this year because the supply chain is not yet mature. Northern Lights followed a different path. It began as a public project, but anchor customers in Norway and then emitters from the Netherlands, Denmark and Sweden turned it into a commercial storage service. The lesson: technologies scale when a buyer and a market route are built in from the start. We are aware that both cases are state-backed flagships; we come back to that under limitations.

### Slide 17 – Three North Sea locations compete for the same role

Our value proposition claims a role for Norway in Europe, so we have to ask who else wants that role. Three locations compete most directly. Aberdeen has the oil and gas supply chain most similar to Norway’s and has leased large areas for floating wind through ScotWind. Esbjerg is Europe’s leading port for installing offshore wind, and Denmark is developing its own CO₂ storage in the North Sea. Rotterdam has Europe’s largest port and industrial cluster and is building CCS infrastructure for its own emitters. Norway cannot beat these locations on volume or cost. Its edge lies elsewhere: cross-border CO₂ storage that is already operating, and floating wind that is already producing power in harsh conditions. That is why we position Norway on proven systems for harsh environments and not on mass manufacturing.

### Slide 18 – Norway as Europe’s reference location for offshore energy systems in harsh environments

From this diagnosis and comparison we derived a national value proposition: Norway as Europe’s reference location for offshore energy systems in harsh environments. The idea is that Norway is the place where these technologies are proven, scaled and then exported. It builds on what is genuinely distinctive: offshore and subsea engineering, harsh-environment operations and safety, and a high-trust culture of collaboration. Following the course, what Norway should not do is just as important. It should not enter a subsidy race for mass manufacturing where it has no cost advantage, it should not duplicate test facilities in every region, and it should avoid permanent operating subsidies without a credible path to competitiveness. Our priorities therefore rest on capabilities. We do not pick winning technologies.

### Slide 19 – One national pipeline from industrial need to first order

This leads to three priorities for ECN. First, convert pilots into first commercial orders. We propose a common six-gate process, from a verified industrial need to international scale-up. The critical gate is number five, the first commercial order, which is exactly where Hywind struggled. So before any project reaches the demonstration stage, it should have a named lead customer, clear purchasing criteria and a procurement route. Second, ECN should run one national pipeline with shared gates and real stop-or-continue decisions, while the regional teams keep their industry relationships. Third, internationalisation should start earlier: export partners and export finance belong in the project before the demonstration is finished, following the Northern Lights model.

### Slide 20 – Who decides, who pays, and why firms take part

A pipeline only works if someone has the authority and the incentives to run it. ECN is a member organisation, so it cannot stop the projects of independent companies. Our proposal is different. ECN prepares the gate reviews and can build on the Technical Committee in Stavanger, which already evaluates innovations technically and commercially for all members. The public funders, Innovation Norway, Enova and the Research Council, then link their co-funding to these gates. So ECN steers where public money goes, not what firms do. Why would an operator commit early as a lead customer? Because the first unit is co-funded, the risk is shared, and the purchasing criteria are developed together with suppliers. ECN can finance the reviews as a member service within the funding model it has to secure by spring 2027. The underlying logic is the stage-gate model from innovation management, applied at cluster level.

### Slide 21 – Recommendations: who needs to do what

Beyond ECN, every actor in the cluster has a role. The national government should turn support into demand: a multi-year, technology-neutral procurement calendar for low-emission offshore energy, with aid tied to a named lead customer and a falling support path. This stays consistent with our principle of not picking winners. Regional and local governments should fast-track ports, land and grid connections and share test sites instead of duplicating them. In the private sector, operators should act as lead customers and publish purchasing criteria, and suppliers should raise business R&D and protect their IP. Universities and institutes should reward IP, spin-outs and industrial PhDs, not only publications. Trade associations can bundle member demand into joint buyer roadmaps and lead export promotion with Norwep. And ECN runs the pipeline, with funders linking their co-funding to the gates.

### Slide 22 – Where economic and societal value grow together

Following Porter and Kramer, we also looked for areas where economic and societal value grow together. In subsea CCS, Norwegian suppliers earn service revenue and build exportable IP, while hard-to-abate industry across Europe cuts its emissions. Northern Lights is the clearest example: it sells CO₂ storage as a service. In floating wind, the economic value lies in engineering, installation and lifecycle services, and the societal value in renewable power and energy security. Hywind Tampen even supplies power to oil and gas platforms and thereby reduces their emissions. In electrification and asset-focused AI, equipment and software suppliers grow while emissions fall and the grid is used better. The practical consequence for ECN: major projects should report both kinds of results, revenue and exports on one side, and verified emission savings and skills on the other.

### Slide 23 – Measure conversion, not activity

To make this concrete, we suggest a roadmap in three phases. In the first year, ECN completes its governance for 2027, maps industrial challenges, defines the gate criteria and records a baseline of its current portfolio. Over the next two years, the focus shifts to challenge-based calls, cross-regional consortia and demonstrations co-funded by industry. After three to five years, the goal is repeat orders and export contracts, and also the discipline to stop weak projects. The scorecard reflects our main message: ECN should measure conversion, not activity. That means the share of projects with a named lead buyer, pilot-to-order rates, export revenue and verified emission savings, in line with Creating Shared Value. Targets should be set once the 2026–2027 baseline is available.

### Slide 24 – What our analysis cannot show yet

Before we conclude, we want to be clear about the limits of our analysis. First, measurement: the innovation output indicators are depressed by Norway’s petroleum-heavy export base, so we read them as signals, not as proof. Second, case selection: Hywind and Northern Lights are both state-backed flagship projects. A counter-case, for example a technology that scaled without a state anchor customer, would test our thesis more strictly. Third, our only primary source is ECN’s own CEO, so the perspective is the institution’s own. Fourth, our Diamond ratings are qualitative. For the final report we therefore add cluster-level statistics and aim for a second interview with a member company. We think the main conclusion holds, but these steps will make it more robust.

### Slide 25 – Norway has the knowledge and the collaboration. What it needs is a reliable path from pilo

To conclude and to answer our research question: Norway is rich, well governed and highly collaborative. But its growth is below average, and its strong knowledge base is not yet turning into new products, intellectual property and high-tech exports. Hywind and Northern Lights show that technical validation alone is not enough. Scaling required an operator-backed deployment path in one case, and anchor customers with cross-border demand in the other. ECN can help by making that path systematic: one national pipeline, shared gates linked to public co-funding, and measurement based on outcomes. That is how Norway can strengthen its position as Europe’s reference location for offshore energy systems in harsh environments. Thank you for your attention. We look forward to your questions.
